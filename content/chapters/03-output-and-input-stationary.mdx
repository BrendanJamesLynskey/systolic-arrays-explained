<Layer kind="concept">

Chapter 2 held the weights still. That is one of three choices for a matrix multiply, each named after the operand that stays put in each PE (the names come from Chen, Emer and Sze's taxonomy for the Eyeriss accelerator, which adds row-stationary for convolutions; SCALE-Sim, a systolic-array simulator, uses these three for matrix multiplies):

- **Weight-stationary** (WS): $B$ stays; $A$ streams in from the left; partial sums move down. A $K \times N$ block of PEs.
- **Output-stationary** (OS): $C$ stays. $a_{mk}$ enters row $m$ from the left and $b_{kn}$ enters column $n$ from the top, both skewed, and they meet in PE$(m, n)$ at cycle $k + m + n$, which adds their product to its own accumulator. Nothing but 8-bit operands moves until the end, when the finished results drain out. An $M \times N$ block.
- **Input-stationary** (IS): $A$ stays (PE$(k, m)$ holds $a_{mk}$); the columns of $B$ stream in from the left; partial sums move down. It is weight-stationary on the transposed problem $C^\top = B^\top A^\top$. A $K \times M$ block.

The animation runs all three on the same <V of="demo.cmp.M" fmt="int" /> × <V of="demo.cmp.K" fmt="int" /> by <V of="demo.cmp.K" fmt="int" /> × <V of="demo.cmp.N" fmt="int" /> problem on one clock, and counts what each moves. When the problem fits the array, all three read every element of $A$ and $B$ from the buffers exactly once (<V of="cmp.ws.reads" fmt="int" /> reads) and write each result once (<V of="cmp.ws.writes" fmt="int" />), and all three make the same number of PE-to-PE hops (<V of="cmp.ws.hops" fmt="int" />). The difference is **what** hops. WS and IS pass a partial sum down at every hop between rows: <V of="cmp.ws.psumHops" fmt="int" /> partial-sum hops here. OS keeps its sums in place and moves them only when draining: <V of="cmp.os.psumHops" fmt="int" />. That matters because partial sums are wide: 8-bit operands accumulate into 32-bit sums. At 128 × 128 × 128, WS moves <V of="big.ws.bits" fmt="int" /> bits between PEs and OS <V of="big.os.bits" fmt="int" />, though OS writes its accumulator registers more often (<V of="big.os.regWrites" fmt="int" /> register writes against <V of="big.ws.regWrites" fmt="int" />).

The bigger difference shows when the problem does not fit, which is the normal case: the stationary matrix is the one that gets reused across a whole stream, and the dataflow decides which matrix is tiled, which is re-read, and where partial sums of a split $K$ go ([chapter 5](/learn/05-tiling)).

</Layer>

Here is output-stationary with the numbers (switch to input-stationary to watch $A$ held and $B$ streamed). Every PE's sky "c" is its accumulator: it gains one term per cycle as the blue $a$ from the left and the vermillion $b$ from above pass through. After the last product the results drain down the columns, one row per cycle.

**This animation is checked against RTL.** The author's [Interview RTL challenge](https://github.com/BrendanJamesLynskey/Interview_RTL_LLM_Accelerators/blob/b37984fc89019d999f6866492433b6cad0977ae8/02_datapath_design/coding_challenges/challenge_01_systolic_array.sv) is a parameterised SystemVerilog output-stationary array with internal skew registers. This site's `rtl/check_rtl.py` runs it in <V of="rtl.simulator" fmt="raw" /> on <V of="rtl.cases" fmt="int" /> problems (including full-range signed INT8) and compares every accumulator after every clock edge with this model: <V of="rtl.values" fmt="int" /> values over <V of="rtl.edges" fmt="int" /> edges, <V of="rtl.mismatches" fmt="int" /> mismatches. (The RTL reads its results out in parallel rather than draining them, so after the last product the check requires it to hold $C$.) The [model page](/model) has the details.

</Layer>

<Layer kind="maths">

**Output-stationary timing.** Row $m$ of $A$ is delayed $m$ cycles and column $n$ of $B$ is delayed $n$ cycles, so $a_{mk}$ enters at cycle $k + m$ and reaches PE$(m, n)$ after $n$ more hops, at $k + m + n$; $b_{kn}$ enters at $k + n$ and reaches PE$(m, n)$ after $m$ hops, at the same cycle. The last product, $k = K-1$ in PE$(M-1, N-1)$, happens at cycle $K + M + N - 3$, so with an $M$-cycle drain

$$
T_{\text{OS}} = (K + M + N - 2) + M .
$$

**Counting the movement.** Reads: every $a_{mk}$ and $b_{kn}$ enters once, $MK + KN$. Operand hops: $a_{mk}$ crosses $N - 1$ links, $b_{kn}$ crosses $M - 1$: $MK(N-1) + KN(M-1)$. Draining moves $c_{mn}$ down $M - 1 - m$ rows: $\tfrac12 N M(M-1)$. For weight-stationary the activation hops are $MK(N-1)$, each $c_{mn}$'s partial sum makes $K - 1$ hops, $MN(K-1)$, and loading moves $w_{kn}$ down $k$ rows, $\tfrac12 NK(K-1)$. With $M = K = N$ both totals are $\tfrac52 N^2 (N-1)$ hops. The model counts every hop in every frame, and the tests check the counts against these closed forms for every dataflow and shape.

</Layer>

<Layer kind="code">

Output-stationary's PE, cut from [`src/lib/sa/model.ts`](https://github.com/BrendanJamesLynskey/systolic-arrays-explained/blob/main/src/lib/sa/model.ts): both operands arrive from neighbours (or the edges), and only the accumulator stays.

```ts
cell[1] = hIn;
cell[2] = vIn;
let acc = prev[mm]![nn]![3]!;
if (hIn !== null && vIn !== null) {
  /* c8 ignore next */
  if (hIn[2] !== vIn[1]) throw new Error("operands out of step");
  cell[4] = [hIn[0], vIn[0]];
  cnt.macs += 1;
  cnt.regWrites += 1;
  acc = [acc[0] + hIn[0] * vIn[0], mm, nn, acc[3] + 1];
}
cell[3] = acc;
```

The check that the two operands belong to the same $k$ never fires: the skew guarantees it, and the tests run every shape.

</Layer>

<Callout kind="illustrative">

The bit counts assume 8-bit operands and 32-bit partial sums (INT8 multiply, INT32 accumulate). The OS drain shifts results down the columns, one row per cycle; the RTL challenge instead reads all accumulators in parallel, and real designs do either. Counts are of values moved, not of energy: what a hop or a register write costs depends on the process and the layout.
